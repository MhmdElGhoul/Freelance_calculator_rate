    How to use this tool

    This calculator finds the lowest hourly rate that still pays you what you want after taxes and business costs. It takes about two minutes.

    1. Start with your take-home goal. Enter the yearly amount you want to keep after expenses and taxes. Think of it as the salary you’d pay yourself to cover rent, savings, and everyday life.
    2. Add your business expenses. Total what you pay each year to run your work: software, equipment, insurance, a workspace, accounting, and marketing. Last year’s bank statements make this easy.
    3. Be realistic about hours. Enter billable hours per week, meaning time you can invoice. Meetings, emails, proposals, and admin usually don’t count, so most freelancers land between 20 and 30. Then enter the weeks you’ll take off for holidays, sick days, and vacation.
    4. Estimate your tax rate. Enter a percentage covering income tax and self-employment contributions. If you’re unsure, ask an accountant or check your tax authority’s guidance.
    5. Read your results. The large figure is your minimum hourly rate. The cushion rate adds 20% to protect you from slow months and late payments. The day rate, monthly target, and colored bar show where each dollar of revenue goes.
    6. Test different scenarios. Change one number at a time. Five more billable hours a week, one cancelled subscription, or one fewer week off can each move your rate noticeably. Results update instantly, so you can compare options before you quote a client.

    Your entries stay in your browser. Nothing is saved or sent anywhere.

    Frequently asked questions

    How is the rate calculated?

    Expenses come off the top, and taxes apply to what’s left as your income. So revenue needed = expenses + take-home income ÷ (1 − tax rate). Divide that by your billable hours for the year (hours per week × weeks worked) to get the hourly rate.

    Why not just divide my salary by 2,080 hours?

    2,080 is a full-time employee’s paid hours. Freelancers spend a lot of time on sales, admin, and learning that nobody pays for, and they cover their own taxes, insurance, and time off. Most freelancers bill 20 to 30 hours a week, so the same income needs a much higher hourly rate.

    How many billable hours should I enter?

    Be conservative. A common range is 20 to 30 hours a week, even when you work 40 or more. If you’re new, or you spend a lot of time finding clients, start near 20. You can raise it later and see the rate drop.

    Which expenses should I include?

    Anything you pay to run the business: software subscriptions, hardware, a workspace, professional insurance, accounting, contractor help, marketing, training, and payment-processing fees. Add a small cushion for surprises, such as a laptop failing.

    What tax rate should I use?

    It depends on your country, income level, and local rules. Many freelancers land between 20% and 35% once self-employment taxes are included. Ask an accountant for a figure that fits your situation, and treat this tool as a planning aid, not tax advice.

    Is this the rate I should quote clients?

    Treat it as your floor. Clients also pay for your experience, speed, and the value of the result, so many freelancers quote 10% to 30% above their minimum. Unpaid gaps between projects, late invoices, and scope creep all eat into the margin, and the extra covers them.

    Should I charge hourly or per project?

    Hourly works well for open-ended or ongoing work. For defined deliverables, a project price is often better: estimate your hours, multiply by your rate, and add a buffer. This way you’re rewarded for working efficiently rather than penalized.
